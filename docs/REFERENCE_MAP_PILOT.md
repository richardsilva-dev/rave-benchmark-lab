# Independent reference map — first education-AI pilot

**Prepared:** 2026-11-16, before the RAVE candidate run.  
**Question:** Benefits, risks and current recommendations for generative AI in education; compare institutional sources and identify evidence limits and policy differences.  
**Status:** Exploratory reference map. It is independent of the RAVE output, but retrospective relative to the already observed Comparison A answer; it is not a preregistered gold standard.

## Primary reference sources

1. **OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026: Exploring Effective Uses of Generative AI in Education* (19 January 2026).** The OECD distinguishes task performance from actual learning: general-purpose GenAI can improve submitted work without producing durable learning gains when cognitive work is outsourced. It argues that purposeful pedagogical design and education-specific tools can support learning, argumentation, tutoring and teacher workflows. Its recommendations include human-centred use, teacher agency, educational R&D, trustworthy governance and equitable infrastructure.  
   https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2026/01/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_940e0dd8.html

2. **UNESCO, *Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research* (2023; Portuguese edition/catalogued guidance in 2024).** The guidance prioritizes human agency, pedagogical appropriateness, age-appropriate use, privacy protection, co-design with educators and learners, and piloting that evaluates effectiveness and long-term impact.  
   https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research  
   https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000390241

3. **Brazilian Ministry of Education / National Education Council, national guidance announced 10–11 November 2026; CNE/CP Opinion 15/2026.** The official MEC notice describes a national framework for all education levels, a four-tier risk classification, human responsibility and oversight, privacy and anti-discrimination safeguards, institutional governance, professional training, and prohibited high-risk practices. It says consequential decisions about assessment, certification, progression and selection remain under human responsibility; AI cannot be the sole or uncorrected grader of essays and other interpretive student work. It also restricts unsupervised direct access for early-years pupils. This is a particularly important current source because it predates the preserved A run (15 November 2026) but was absent from the A source list.  
   https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2026/novembro/mec-estabelece-diretrizes-para-uso-responsavel-da-ia-na-educacao

4. **MEC, *Inteligência Artificial na Educação Básica* (8 April 2026).** The 88-page document is an official curricular and ethical-use guide for Brazilian basic education. Its stated scope distinguishes learning *about* AI from learning *with* AI and asks schools to evaluate when, how and under what conditions AI should be used.  
   https://www.gov.br/mec/pt-br/escolas-conectadas/arquivos/ia-basica.pdf

5. **Unicamp, *Guia de IA na Unicamp* and Deliberação CONSU-A-005/2026.** The university frames AI as support rather than replacement; requires human review and accountability; emphasizes data protection, academic integrity, transparency, bias awareness and context-appropriate use. Its institutional rules include explicit declaration requirements for relevant academic work.  
   https://inteligenciaartificial.unicamp.br/guia-de-ia-na-unicamp/  
   https://inteligenciaartificial.unicamp.br/politicas-e-normas/

6. **McDonald, Johri, Ali & Hingle Collier (2025), analysis of 116 US R1 university policy sets.** The study found varied institutional approaches: 63% of sampled institutions encouraged GenAI use, 27% discouraged it and 10% were intermediate. Guidance differed by task, classroom practice and ethical emphasis. Important limitation: the documents were collected in October–November 2023 and the sample was restricted to US R1 institutions; the findings are evidence of policy variation, not a current global prevalence estimate.  
   https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2025.100121

## Reference checklist for answer coverage

| Dimension | Expected elements to look for | Evidence standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Potential benefits | Explanations, tutoring, feedback, accessibility/support, teacher planning and institutional workflows | State the pedagogical conditions; do not infer durable learning from task performance alone |
| Learning risks | Cognitive offloading, reduced independent thinking/metacognition, shallow task completion, dependence | Distinguish measured learning outcomes from performance or convenience |
| Reliability risks | Hallucinations, outdated or fabricated claims/references, bias and discrimination | Require verification and human accountability |
| Privacy and rights | Personal/sensitive data, confidentiality, intellectual property, authorship and transparency | Identify data governance and disclosure obligations |
| Equity and age | Unequal access to devices/connectivity, accessibility, age-appropriate use and supervision | Explain who may be disadvantaged and what safeguards are proposed |
| Governance | Human oversight, risk classification, professional training, clear institutional policies, monitoring and accountability | Separate international guidance, national rules and institution-level policy |
| Policy differences | UNESCO/OECD principles; Brazil's national risk-based guidance; Unicamp's institutional requirements; variation across US university policies | Describe differences in scope and specificity; do not claim contradiction without direct evidence |
| Evidence limitations | Short follow-up, limited causal evidence, lack of long-term outcomes, rapidly changing tools, regional/context differences | Explicitly mark what is not yet known |

## Current policy divergence to recognize

The sources do not present one universal permission rule. UNESCO and OECD provide broad human-centred and pedagogical principles. Brazil's November 2026 MEC/CNE framework adds national risk tiers, prohibitions and human-responsibility rules. Unicamp translates principles into institution-specific requirements, including disclosure. The 2025 policy study documents variation among US institutions, but its 2023 sample cannot be generalized to all countries or to current 2026 policies. These are differences in jurisdiction, date and level of governance, not necessarily substantive contradictions.

## Known coverage gap in the preserved A answer

The completed A result listed eight sources but did not cite the Brazilian MEC/CNE national guidelines announced on 10 November 2026, despite the source being available before the 15 November run. The A answer also describes benefits such as personalization and higher-order skills broadly; the OECD reference requires a sharper distinction between improved task performance and demonstrated learning gains. These are candidate omissions to check in the blinded outputs, not automatic score deductions until the full answer and source traceability are reviewed.
